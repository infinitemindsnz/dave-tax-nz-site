Many family-run businesses look profitable on paper, but that impression can be misleading. In an article for Home Voice, tax barrister Dave Ananth of Meridian Partners explains that when family members work long hours without receiving standard market wages, the business's reported profit often masks an underlying reality: the business may not actually be sustainable once labor is properly costed.

According to Ananth, the issue becomes especially clear when a business is put up for sale. Prospective buyers do not simply look at the profit figures a family has been reporting. Instead, they assess the business's true, sustainable earning capacity by working out what it would cost to replace every unpaid or underpaid family worker with employees paid at market rates. Once that adjustment is made, a business that appeared healthy can turn out to be barely breaking even, or worse.

The article points to a related problem: businesses that fail to properly record wages, or that rely on unrecorded cash payments to family members, are taking on significant risk. Ananth notes that this kind of informal arrangement can expose a business to serious consequences, including back-tax audits from Inland Revenue, penalties for failing to comply with wage obligations, and disputes within the family itself when expectations around pay and contribution are not clearly documented.

The core message of the piece is that paying children and other family members fair, market-rate wages, and keeping transparent financial records, is not just a compliance matter. Ananth argues that it also instills financial discipline within the business, helps protect trust among family members, and produces a valuation that a genuine buyer can rely on. A business built on undocumented or underpaid family labor may look attractive at first glance, but Ananth's central point is that such a foundation rarely holds up to scrutiny, whether that scrutiny comes from Inland Revenue, from a prospective buyer, or eventually from the family members themselves.

For family business owners, the practical takeaway from the article is straightforward: treat family employees the way you would treat any other employee when it comes to pay and record-keeping. Doing so may reduce the paper profit in the short term, but it produces a more honest, defensible, and ultimately more valuable picture of how the business actually performs.
