## A growing overseas debt problem

In an opinion piece published on interest.co.nz on 31 August 2026, tax specialist Dave Ananth examines how Inland Revenue can use section 145A of the Student Loan Scheme Act 2011 to address ballooning student loan balances owed by New Zealanders who have moved overseas.

Ananth points to the scale of the enforcement effort behind this issue, noting that IRD made 73,732 file referrals to overseas debt collectors across the 2022/23 to 2025/26 financial years. That volume of referrals, he suggests, illustrates both the size of the overseas debt book and the limits of relying purely on collection agencies to chase down decades-old balances.

## Why traditional enforcement falls short

According to Ananth, conventional recovery methods often produce disappointing results when the debt in question has been accumulating for many years. Interest and penalties can compound a loan balance well beyond the original amount borrowed, making the total figure appear unmanageable to the borrower and, in practice, difficult for the Crown to actually recover. He argues that pressure from debt collectors alone does not necessarily translate into money in IRD's hands, particularly where borrowers have become disengaged from the system entirely.

## What section 145A actually allows

Ananth explains that section 145A, read together with related provisions in the Student Loan Scheme Act 2011, gives the Commissioner of Inland Revenue discretionary power to write off equitable amounts of ordinary loan interest and to cancel accrued interest in appropriate cases. This discretion is not triggered automatically. Rather, it becomes available once a borrower takes a number of specific steps: re-engaging with IRD after a period of absence, making full and honest disclosure of their financial position, and putting forward a realistic proposal to settle the debt, whether through a lump sum payment or an instalment arrangement.

## Not an amnesty, but a negotiated resolution

A central point in Ananth's piece is that this mechanism should not be mistaken for a blanket amnesty or an automatic discount. The relief under section 145A is discretionary and conditional, meaning it depends on the individual borrower's willingness to come forward, be transparent about their circumstances, and commit to a workable repayment plan. Ananth is careful to frame the provision as a tool for resolving debt on reasonable terms rather than a mechanism for borrowers to simply have their obligations reduced without engagement.

## A practical middle ground

Drawing on his own professional experience, Ananth observes that when borrowers do come forward with complete financial disclosure and a credible settlement proposal, the outcome can benefit both sides. The Crown is able to recover substantial sums that might otherwise remain permanently uncollectable from someone living overseas and effectively out of reach of standard enforcement action. At the same time, the borrower gains the ability to finally close out a historical debt that may have been growing, unresolved, for years.

## Why this matters for overseas borrowers

For borrowers who left New Zealand with student loan balances still outstanding, Ananth's explanation of section 145A highlights a potential pathway back into good standing with IRD. Rather than continuing to avoid contact and allow interest to accumulate indefinitely, the provision offers a structured route to negotiate a resolution, provided the borrower is prepared to be transparent and realistic about what they can afford to repay.

Ananth's piece, published as an opinion article on interest.co.nz, is intended to inform borrowers and advisers about how this specific statutory discretion operates in practice, rather than to serve as a guarantee of relief in any individual case.
