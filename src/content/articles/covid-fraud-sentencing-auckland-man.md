An Auckland man has been sentenced to six months community detention for fraud connected to a COVID-19 relief scheme, according to a report by Inland Revenue, written by Shankar.

Martin Kenneth Kells appeared for sentencing in the Auckland District Court on 11 September, facing a representative charge of dishonestly using a document to obtain money he was not entitled to. Kells, now a salary and wage earner, was previously a director and trustee of several entities.

The case centres on the Small Business Cashflow Scheme (SBCS), a loan scheme announced by the government in April 2020 as part of its COVID-19 economic response. The scheme operated on a "high trust" model, designed to give businesses fast access to funds to help them survive the pandemic. To qualify, applicants needed to show a minimum 30 percent decline in actual or predicted revenue between January and June 2020, and their business had to have existed before 1 April 2020.

According to the report, Kells submitted six separate SBCS applications in his own name and in the names of related companies and a trust, despite knowing that the eligibility declarations in each application were false. Through this conduct he received a total of $70,800, with funds paid either into his personal bank account or into an account under his control.

Rather than using the funds to support legitimate businesses, the money was reportedly spent on groceries, school fees, credit card payments and alcohol, with some also transferred to his ex-wife. Kells has made no voluntary repayments himself, although the report notes that his brother repaid one loan of $11,800.

Kells sought a discharge without conviction, but this application was declined. In assessing the seriousness of the offending, Judge Jelas reportedly accepted that anxiety, depression, the pressures of COVID-19 restrictions, and financial strain from debt all contributed to his decision to act dishonestly.

On the question of the consequences of a conviction, Her Honour accepted that it could affect Kells' ability to travel, but noted that a conviction was not the only matter he might need to disclose to overseas authorities, pointing to his existing bankruptcy. Judge Jelas concluded that the consequences of a conviction were not disproportionate to the gravity of the offending.

In addition to the community detention sentence, Kells was ordered to repay the remaining core debt of $59,000 by 18 September.

The case stands as a reminder of the scrutiny being applied to COVID-19 relief scheme claims, even years after the schemes were introduced on a high-trust basis to support businesses through the pandemic.
