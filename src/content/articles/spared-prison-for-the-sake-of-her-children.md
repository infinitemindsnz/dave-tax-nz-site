A recent sentencing in an Auckland court has highlighted how personal and family circumstances can influence outcomes even in serious cases of tax fraud. According to a report published via ird.govt.nz and credited to Thenna Ashok, Judge Alofivae sentenced a husband and wife to very different outcomes despite finding them equally responsible for the offending.

Faalili Vaa and his wife, Belinda Heilupe Vaa, faced representative charges of evading income tax and GST, and of using a document to dishonestly obtain income tax advantages and a Small Business Cashflow Scheme loan. The offending spanned nine years, from 2015 to 2024, and related to the couple's kitchen installation business.

According to the report, both Vaa and his wife benefited from the business profits and were aware that GST and income tax returns filed on their behalf understated sales and inflated expenses. The report also states that once Inland Revenue began reviewing their tax affairs in 2018, the couple deliberately stopped filing GST and income tax returns altogether. In total, the evasion and fraud — covering income tax, GST, and the Small Business Cashflow Scheme loan — amounted to $554,998.68.

Judge Alofivae set a starting point of 44 months' imprisonment for both defendants. After discounts for guilty pleas, remorse, and the couple's difficult home circumstances raising autistic children, that starting point was reduced to 26 months' imprisonment for each.

However, the judge went further for Belinda Vaa, reducing her sentence to 24 months before converting it to 12 months' home detention. The report notes that Judge Alofivae described this as a decision made strictly on humanitarian grounds, given the impact that imprisoning both parents would have on their high-needs children. Faalili Vaa, by contrast, was sentenced to 26 months' imprisonment.

The report also records concerns raised by the judge about Faalili Vaa continuing to work as a self-employed tradesperson despite facing these charges. Judge Alofivae reportedly described the nine years of offending as "absolutely unacceptable" and suggested that he had drawn his wife into the offending. She also noted concerns, drawn from a pre-sentence report, that he did not appear to fully grasp the seriousness of what he had done. The judge is reported as having rejected any suggestion that ignorance of tax obligations could serve as a defence.

Cases like this illustrate the weight that courts can give to family hardship alongside the scale of financial offending. While the underlying conduct — years of deliberately falsified returns and deliberate non-filing once under review — was treated as serious and sustained, the court's sentencing approach shows how individual circumstances, including the wellbeing of dependent children, can shape outcomes even where culpability is otherwise shared equally between offenders.

For business owners, the case is a reminder that tax obligations apply regardless of family circumstances, and that Inland Revenue reviews can uncover years of inaccurate reporting. Genuine remorse, cooperation, and early guilty pleas appear to have been treated as mitigating factors in this case, but they did not prevent a lengthy prison sentence for one of the two offenders.
